### Working papers

 * History written by the Loser: Centralization of Confederate Narratives in Southern History Textbooks, *with Pedro Aldighieri*, *(draft available upon request)*

   <details>
   <summary>Abstract</summary>
   <br>
   <blockquote style="text-align: justify;">
     This paper examines how Southern states exploited centralization of textbook adoption lists to influence narratives in American history textbooks between 1870 and 1930. We employ large language models to  measure pro-Southern slant on a novel dataset of 405 editions of middle-school history textbooks. Leveraging variation in the timing of state adoption laws, we find that state-level consolidation led to 21 p.p. more pro-Confederate coverage of the Civil War. This reflected in more favorable portrayals of the Old South and Confederate apologetics in war descriptions. We show that adoption laws reshaped the publishing industry by enabling new Southern publishers to enter the market and pushing big established Northern publishers to launch new titles with more pro-Southern content. Exploiting variation in the timing of adoption laws, we show that Southern men from cohorts exposed to these textbooks were more likely to marry South-born women and to support KKK and lynching.
   </blockquote>
   </details>

 * <p>
  <a href="https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/n8py72n762g3lbjfw3ss0/eliseeva_male_scarcity.pdf?rlkey=i5as07ss66kseutfkgyqmzkke&amp;raw=1"
     target="_blank" rel="noopener">
    Failing to forge the New Soviet Woman: Long-term effect of WW2-induced male scarcity on family formation
  </a><br>
</p>
   
   <details>
   <summary>Abstract</summary>
   <br>
   <blockquote style="text-align: justify;">
     Does male scarcity empower women, or, conversely, push them to conform to traditional gender roles? Existing work finds that male scarcity expands women's autonomy. Drawing on the episode of male scarcity caused by 27 million Soviet casualties during WWII, I find that it entrenched family-oriented societal norms and increased family formation in the long run. I exploit variation in the 1959 female share across regions and birth cohorts, and instrument it with region-cohort military fatality rates built from 11 million draft records. In the short run male scarcity worsened women's marriage prospects: exposed cohorts married less, remarried less often, and had fewer children. Yet, the exposed generation was more likely to see marriage as a lifelong commitment and two-parent families as the norm, a preference they could not act on in a thin marriage market but transmitted to their children. In the next generation the marriage and fertility effects reverse: children of exposed parents marry more and have more children, and they divorce less, as their parents did. Female employment responded to male scarcity only briefly, and attitudes quickly turned against women's employment. Overall, male scarcity entrenches family-oriented norms where no empowerment through participation in the labor force is possible, and state propaganda holds the family's importance in place.
   </blockquote>
   </details>
